# Appendix C: Pay per crawl and content licensing

Chapter 4 treats access for each kind of crawler as a yes or a no. This appendix covers the step in between: yes, for a fee. It sits here, and not in the main text, because it is a business decision that changes fast, and because most sites in this book (Nordlys Cabins included) have no reason to charge agents for reading. If your content is the product, start here after Chapter 4: verify identities first, measure who reads what, and only then put a price on it.

## Pay per crawl, HTTP 402 and licensing

Once you can tell operators apart reliably, a new question appears: could some of them pay? This is where most of the hype in this area lives, so let's be precise about what exists in November 2026.

**Pay per crawl** is Cloudflare's product, announced on 1 July 2025. A site sets a price per request, and a crawler without an agreement gets `HTTP 402 Payment Required` with a `crawler-price` header. A crawler that wants to pay sends `crawler-exact-price` or `crawler-max-price` and gets the content with a `crawler-charged` header. Crawlers must authenticate with Web Bot Auth, which is exactly why the signature matters here: the site needs to know whom to charge.[^appC-1] As of Cloudflare's documentation updated on 28 July 2026, pay per crawl was still in closed beta, with Cloudflare acting as merchant of record.[^appC-2] Cloudflare publishes no adoption numbers for it.

**x402** and **MPP** are the open protocols that use the same status code for agents paying per API call. The next section explains both. Note that pay per crawl and x402 use different, incompatible headers, and that HTTP itself reserves 402 "for future use" without defining it in detail.

**RSL** (Really Simple Licensing) 1.0 is a specification published on 10 December 2025 by a group of publishers and platforms, with editors from the RSL Collective, Condé Nast, Ziff Davis, Schema.org, Yahoo, Automattic, O'Reilly and Fastly. It describes licensing terms for content in an XML file that `robots.txt` points to with a `License:` line.[^appC-3] It is a way to *state* terms. Enforcing them still needs the identity and edge tools from this chapter.

**Commercial intermediaries** such as TollBit sell the same idea as a service. TollBit's own site offers publishers a "Bot Paywall" to "set your terms for paid AI access" and "Licensed RAG Access" to "license content for AI retrieval".[^appC-4] Their commercial terms are not public, so read them before you sign.

What does Nordlys do with all this? Nothing, and I think that is the right answer for most booking sites, shops and SaaS products. Nordlys earns money when an agent books a cabin. Charging the agent for reading the cabin page would only make it recommend someone else's cabin. Pay per crawl and RSL make sense when the content *is* the product: news, reference works, research, recipes, stock photos. If that is your business, the order of work is the same as here: verify identities first, measure who reads what (Chapter 12), and only then put a price on it.

## Paying per request: x402 and MPP

The last group answers question three for a different kind of purchase: an agent paying for one API call or one document, a few cents at a time, with no account and no checkout page. Both protocols revive HTTP status code 402 Payment Required, which the HTTP specifications reserved long ago and never defined in detail.

**x402** came out of Coinbase, and Cloudflare and Coinbase announced an x402 Foundation together in September 2025.[^appC-5] On 2 April 2026 the foundation launched under the Linux Foundation, and by its operational launch on 14 July 2026 it had 40 members, with Adyen, AWS, American Express, Circle, Cloudflare, Coinbase, Fiserv, Google, Mastercard, Shopify, Stripe and Visa among the premier ones.[^appC-6] Version 2 of the protocol uses three headers, each carrying base64-encoded JSON: `PAYMENT-REQUIRED` from server to client with the 402, `PAYMENT-SIGNATURE` from client to server on the retry, and `PAYMENT-RESPONSE` from server to client with the settlement result.[^appC-7] The `PAYMENT-REQUIRED` object lists what the server `accepts`: a scheme (such as `exact`), a network in CAIP-2 format (such as `eip155:84532`, the Base Sepolia test network), an amount in atomic units of an asset, and the address to pay.[^appC-8] In practice that means stablecoins on blockchains today, which is why I call x402 crypto-first.

**MPP**, the Machine Payments Protocol, is the payment industry’s other answer. Stripe’s documentation describes it as “an open protocol that lets agents pay for your APIs and services programmatically without a checkout UI, co-authored by Stripe and Tempo”.[^appC-9] It launched on 18 March 2026 alongside the Tempo blockchain’s mainnet (Stripe’s announcement and several reports give that date).[^appC-10] Where x402 invents its own headers, MPP defines a new HTTP authentication scheme called `Payment`: the 402 carries a `WWW-Authenticate: Payment ...` challenge with an id, a realm, a payment method and an encoded request, and the client answers with a credential.[^appC-11] Its core is an individual Internet-Draft, “The ‘Payment’ HTTP Authentication Scheme” (`draft-httpauth-payment-01`, last updated 9 September 2026), with no working group behind it yet.[^appC-12] MPP supports cards through Stripe’s Shared Payment Tokens as well as stablecoins, and its own blog announced x402 compatibility in June 2026.[^appC-9][^appC-11]

Neither is for Nordlys. A cabin booking is a large, considered purchase with a cancellation policy, not a per-request micropayment. These protocols are for API businesses and for content publishers. Cloudflare's pay per crawl, above, uses a 402 of its own. If you want to see a 402 response on the wire, the demos include a teaching example, `nordlys-cabins/layers/11-commerce/x402-demo.js`, with a route, `/guide/premium/winter-routes`, that exists only when you start the app with `NORDLYS_X402_DEMO=1`. It answers with an x402 V2 `PAYMENT-REQUIRED` challenge that uses the test-network values from the specification's own example and an all-zeros `payTo` address.

## Reserving text and data mining rights: TDMRep

If your organisation operates in the European Union (or the wider European Economic Area), your legal team may ask about a reservation of rights for text and data mining. Article 4 of the EU Directive on Copyright in the Digital Single Market allows text and data mining of lawfully accessible content unless the rightholder has reserved that right "in an appropriate manner, such as machine-readable means". The EU AI Act then requires providers of general-purpose AI models to have a copyright policy that identifies and complies with such reservations, "including through state-of-the-art technologies" (Article 53(1)(c)).[^appC-13] The voluntary General-Purpose AI Code of Practice, published on 10 July 2025, adds commitments for signatories' crawlers, including honouring `robots.txt`.[^appC-14]

Whether a given line in a given file counts as an "appropriate" reservation is a legal question, and this book is not legal advice. What I can describe is the technical menu:

- The Content Signals comment block frames `ai-train=no` as an Article 4 reservation.
- The W3C TDM Reservation Protocol (TDMRep), a Community Group report from 2024, expresses the reservation as a JSON file at `/.well-known/tdmrep.json` (registered with IANA), as an HTTP header or as an HTML meta tag. The opt-out is `tdm-reservation` set to `1`, for example `[{"location": "/", "tdm-reservation": 1}]`, optionally with a `tdm-policy` URL pointing to your licensing terms.[^appC-15]
- `robots.txt` rules for training crawlers, which the Code of Practice commits signatories to respect.

TDMRep covers text and data mining in general, not only AI training, so check with legal what you actually want to reserve before you publish it. Nordlys does not publish a `tdmrep.json`. The team's "no" to training is a preference, not a licensing business, and the `robots.txt` groups plus Content Signals express it well enough for its purposes.
